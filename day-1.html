MODULE 1 · FUNDAMENTALS

What is avian behaviour?

Behaviour includes the observable actions and responses of birds to internal states and external conditions.

  • Environmental conditions
  • Physiology and internal state
  • Learning and experience
  • Social interactions
Think: What can you learn by watching a bird for five minutes?

MODULE 1 · ECOLOGY

Behaviour responds to context

What can influence behaviour?

  • Food availability
  • Predation risk
  • Competitors or potential mates
  • Temperature, rainfall, light and season

MODULE 3 · DOCUMENTING

Make observations comparable

An ethogram is a structured list of behaviours with clear, operational definitions.

Good definitions describe observable actions—not assumed emotions or motivations.

FeedingManipulating or consuming an apparent food item.

PreeningUsing the bill to arrange or clean feathers.

LocomotionMoving from one location to another.

VigilanceA defined pause or posture used to scan surroundings.

MODULE 3 · ACTIVITY

Build a mini-ethogram

A bird pecks at the ground, scans its surroundings, preens and moves to another patch.

Possible answer

Feeding: pecking at or manipulating an apparent food item; count pecks or measure feeding-bout duration.

Vigilance: a defined pause in feeding with a scanning posture; measure duration if bout boundaries are clear.

MODULE 4 · MIGRATION

What counts as migration?

Migration is recurring movement between areas, often seasonal, that may connect breeding and non-breeding locations.

  • Routes and distances vary
  • Timing differs between individuals
  • Some populations are only partly migratory
Migration is not only about escaping cold: seasonal food and breeding opportunities also matter.

MODULE 4 · MOVEMENT TYPES

Different ways birds move

Dispersal

Movement away from a birthplace or previous breeding site.

Nomadism

Movement that tracks changing or unpredictable resources.

Irruption

Irregular large-scale movement beyond usual patterns.

Altitudinal movement

Movement between elevations.

These categories can overlap; real movement patterns do not always fit neat boxes.

MODULE 4 · MIGRATION PATTERNS

Migration is not one-size-fits-all

↗

Long-distance

Movement between distant seasonal ranges.

↕

Short-distance / altitudinal

Movement over shorter distances or between elevations.

½

Only part of a population migrates.

⇢

Differential migration

Groups differ in route, distance or non-breeding area.

Why stopovers matter

Birds may need places to rest and refuel. Conditions at one stopover can affect the rest of the journey, connecting habitats that face very different threats.

MODULE 5 · WHY MIGRATE?

Why leave a place that works?

A place that is suitable now may not remain suitable throughout the year.

Potential benefits

  • Seasonal food availability
  • Favourable breeding conditions
  • Reduced exposure to harsh conditions
  • Greater reproductive opportunity

Potential costs

  • High energy demands
  • Predation and bad weather
  • Risky stopovers
  • Time and opportunities lost during travel
Migration can be favoured when its effects on survival and reproduction outweigh the costs relative to alternatives.

MODULE 5 · ACTIVITY

Does food explain migration?

Shorebirds arrive at a coastal wetland each winter after travelling long distances.

Question: What evidence would help test whether seasonal food availability influences their use of the wetland?

Possible evidence
  • Measure prey abundance across seasons and sites.
  • Record bird abundance using comparable survey effort.
  • Measure feeding rates or body condition where appropriate.
  • Compare sites or years with different prey availability.

A large number of birds at a food-rich site is suggestive, but does not prove food caused migration.
