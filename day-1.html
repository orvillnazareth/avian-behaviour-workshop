Observe

Investigate

01 · FUNDAMENTALS

What is avian behaviour?

Behaviour includes the actions and responses of birds to their internal state and external environment.

Behaviour is shaped by…

  • Environmental conditions
  • Physiology and internal state
  • Learning and experience
  • Social interactions

Opening question

When you watch a bird for five minutes, what can you learn about it?

Observation is the starting point. Explanation needs further investigation.

01 · FUNDAMENTALS

Major categories of behaviour

Finding and communicating

  • Foraging
  • Calls, songs and displays
  • Social interactions
  • Territorial behaviour

Maintaining and reproducing

  • Preening and resting
  • Courtship and mating
  • Incubation
  • Parental care

Categories can overlap. Context matters: a call may serve different functions in different situations.

01 · FUNDAMENTALS

Behaviour, ecology and environment

What might change behaviour?

  • Food availability
  • Predation risk
  • Competitors or mates
  • Temperature, rainfall, light and season

Example: a receding tide

Shorebirds may feed more as mudflats become exposed and prey becomes accessible.

This is a possible explanation—not proof that food alone caused the pattern.

02 · TINBERGEN

Four ways to ask “why?”

Proximate questions

Mechanism: What triggers or controls the behaviour?

Development: How does it develop during an individual’s life?

Ultimate questions

Function: How might it affect survival or reproduction?

Evolution: How has it changed over evolutionary history?

These questions complement one another; one explanation rarely answers all four.

03 · DOCUMENTING BEHAVIOUR

Make observations comparable.

An ethogram is a structured list of behaviours with clear, operational definitions.

BehaviourExample definition
FeedingManipulating or consuming an apparent food item
PreeningUsing the bill to arrange or clean feathers
LocomotionMoving from one location to another
VigilanceA defined pause or posture used to scan surroundings

Record what you can observe—not an assumed emotion or motivation.

03 · MEASUREMENT

Events, states and activity budgets

MeasureExamplesUseful for
EventsCalls, pecks, prey capturesCounts or rates
StatesFeeding, resting, incubationDuration
Activity budgetTime in each behaviourProportion of observed time

Example: 9 minutes feeding during a 20-minute observation = 45% of observed time.

Activity budgets describe time allocation; they do not by themselves explain why behaviour occurs.

03 · ACTIVITY

Build a mini-ethogram

A bird pecks at the ground, scans its surroundings, preens and moves to another patch.

Possible answer

Feeding: pecking at or manipulating an apparent food item; can be counted as events or recorded as feeding-bout duration.

Vigilance: a defined pause in feeding with a scanning posture; can be measured as duration if bout boundaries are clear.

Definitions should fit the species and research question.

04 · MIGRATION

What counts as migration?

Migration is a recurring movement between areas, often seasonal, that may connect breeding and non-breeding locations.

Patterns can vary by…

  • Distance and route
  • Timing and duration
  • Which individuals move
  • Predictability of destinations

Related movement terms

Dispersal: movement away from a birthplace or previous breeding site.

Nomadism: movement tracking changing resources.

Irruption: irregular large-scale movement beyond usual patterns.

04 · MIGRATION

Migration is not one-size-fits-all.

Long-distance

Movement between distant seasonal ranges.

Short-distance or altitudinal

Movement over shorter distances or between elevations.

Partial migration

Only part of a population migrates.

Differential migration

Groups differ in routes, distances or non-breeding areas.

A species label alone does not tell us exactly where every individual goes.

04 · MIGRATION

Migration connects places.

  1. Breeding period
  2. Post-breeding movement
  3. Migration and stopovers
  4. Non-breeding period
  5. Return migration

Why stopovers matter

Birds may need places to rest and refuel. Conditions at stopovers can affect the rest of the journey.

Different locations along a route can expose birds to different threats.

05 · WHY MIGRATE?

Why leave a place that works?

A place that is suitable now may not remain suitable throughout the year.

Potential benefits

  • Seasonal food availability
  • Favourable breeding conditions
  • Reduced exposure to harsh conditions
  • Greater reproductive opportunity

Potential costs

  • High energy demands
  • Predation and bad weather
  • Risky stopovers
  • Time and opportunities lost during travel

Migration can be favoured when its effects on survival and reproduction outweigh the costs relative to alternatives.

05 · ACTIVITY

Does food explain migration?

Shorebirds arrive at a coastal wetland each winter after travelling long distances. What evidence would help test whether seasonal food availability influences their use of the wetland?

Possible evidence
  • Measure prey abundance across seasons and sites.
  • Record bird abundance using comparable survey effort.
  • Measure feeding rates or body condition where appropriate.
  • Compare sites or years with different prey availability.

A large number of birds at a food-rich site is suggestive, but it does not prove food caused migration.
