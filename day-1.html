MODULE 01 · THE BIG PICTURE

There is more than one
way to be a bird.

From elaborate courtship displays to long-distance navigation, birds challenge our assumptions about animal behaviour.

Notice

What is the bird doing? Describe the behaviour without explaining it yet.

Investigate

What could explain it? What evidence would help us distinguish between explanations?

Opening: You can introduce the workshop by noting that birds show remarkable diversity in behaviour, cognition, communication and movement. If using Jennifer Ackerman's writing as inspiration, quote only a short attributed excerpt and paraphrase the rest. Ask participants to name one bird behaviour they find surprising.

MODULE 01 · FUNDAMENTALS

What is avian behaviour?

Behaviour includes the actions and responses of birds to their internal state and external environment.

Behaviour is shaped by…

  • Environmental conditions
  • Physiology and internal state
  • Learning and experience
  • Social interactions

When you watch a bird for five minutes, what can you learn about it?

Observation is the starting point. Explanation requires further investigation.

Invite two or three answers. Emphasise that we can record what we see, but a short observation rarely reveals the whole explanation. Avoid defining behaviour as only movement: calls and other observable responses also count.

MODULE 01 · FUNDAMENTALS

Major categories of behaviour

  • Foraging: searching for, handling and consuming food
  • Communication: calls, songs, displays and signals
  • Social and territorial: flocking, aggression, spacing
  • Maintenance: preening, bathing and resting
  • Reproductive: courtship and mating
  • Parental care: incubation, feeding and protection

Categories can overlap. A call may function in communication, territory defence or contact—context matters.

Use familiar examples. Ask whether a bird calling is always defending territory. The answer is no: context, call type and the receiver's response matter.

MODULE 01 · FUNDAMENTALS

Behaviour, ecology and environment

What might change a bird's behaviour?

  • Food availability
  • Predation risk
  • Presence of competitors or mates
  • Temperature, rainfall, light and season

Example: a receding tide

Shorebirds may increase feeding as mudflats become exposed and prey becomes accessible.

This is a possible explanation—not proof that food alone caused the pattern.

Stress that behaviour is responsive to multiple influences. One observation may generate a hypothesis, but repeated observations or a suitable comparison are needed to test it.

MODULE 01 · QUICK ACTIVITY

Observe first. Explain second.

A bird pecks at the ground, pauses to look around, then flies into nearby vegetation when another bird approaches.

Discuss

  1. What did you directly observe?
  2. What are two possible explanations?
  3. What additional evidence would help?
Allow about two minutes. Example observations: pecking, pausing, looking around, flying into vegetation after another bird approaches. Possible interpretations include avoiding aggression, responding to disturbance, or moving to another food patch. Ask what evidence could distinguish them.

MODULE 02 · UNDERSTANDING BEHAVIOUR

Four ways to ask
“why?”

Tinbergen's four questions help us investigate different parts of a behavioural explanation.

Proximate questions

Mechanism: What triggers or controls it?

Development: How does it develop during an individual's life?

Ultimate questions

Function: How might it affect survival or reproduction?

Evolution: How has it changed across evolutionary history?

Use the male paradise flycatcher's long tail as an example. Ask what cues trigger display, how the tail and display develop, whether the display influences mating success, and how related species differ. Explain that proximate and ultimate explanations complement rather than replace each other.

MODULE 02 · KNOWLEDGE CHECK

Which question is it?

A researcher investigates whether males with more elaborate courtship displays attract more mates. Which of Tinbergen's questions is the main focus?

Choose an answer to see feedback.

Give participants time to answer. The focus is function because the study examines a potential consequence for reproductive success. A real study would need to account for other differences between males and measure mating success carefully.

MODULE 03 · DOCUMENTING BEHAVIOUR

Make observations comparable.

Two observers should be able to use the same definitions and record behaviour consistently.

Ethogram

A structured list of behaviours with clear, operational definitions.

Example definitions

Feeding: manipulating or consuming food.

Preening: using the bill to arrange or clean feathers.

Locomotion: moving from one location to another.

Explain that definitions should be observable and specific. “Looks nervous” is an interpretation; a defined posture or action is easier to record consistently. Definitions may need adapting to the study species and context.

MODULE 03 · MEASUREMENT

Events, states and activity budgets

MeasureExamplesUseful for
EventsCalls, pecks, prey capturesCounts or rates
StatesFeeding, resting, incubationDuration
Activity budgetTime spent in each behaviourProportion of observed time

Example: 9 minutes feeding during a 20-minute observation = 45% of observed time.

Activity budgets describe time allocation; they do not, by themselves, explain why a bird behaves that way.

Mention that some behaviours can be recorded as either events or states depending on the research question and operational definitions. Explain the calculation: behaviour duration divided by total observation time, multiplied by 100.

MODULE 03 · QUICK ACTIVITY

Build a mini-ethogram

A bird pecks at the ground, scans its surroundings, preens and moves to another patch.

Write operational definitions for any two behaviours. Decide whether you would record each as an event, a state, or both.

Possible answer

Feeding: bird pecks at or manipulates an apparent food item; record as an event count or as a feeding bout duration.

Vigilance: bird pauses feeding and holds its head/body in a defined scanning posture; record duration if the bout boundaries are clear.

These are examples. Definitions should fit the species and study question.

Give one or two minutes for participants to write. Encourage observable definitions, not inferred motivation. There is no single universal ethogram; it is built for the research question.

MODULE 04 · AVIAN MIGRATION

What counts as migration?

A recurring movement between areas, often seasonal, that may connect breeding and non-breeding locations.

Migration can vary by…

  • Distance and route
  • Timing and duration
  • Which individuals move
  • Whether destinations are predictable

Not every movement is migration

Dispersal, nomadic movements, irruptions and altitudinal movements describe different patterns, although categories can overlap.

Ask participants to name a migratory bird. Emphasise that migration is not only about escaping cold: seasonal food, breeding opportunities and other ecological factors matter. Briefly define dispersal as movement away from a birthplace or previous breeding site; nomadism tracks changing resources; irruptions are irregular large movements.

MODULE 04 · MIGRATION PATTERNS

Migration is not one-size-fits-all.

Long-distance

Movement between distant seasonal ranges.

Short-distance or altitudinal

Movement over shorter distances or between elevations.

Partial migration

Only part of a population migrates.

Differential migration

Groups may differ in route, distance or non-breeding areas.

A species label alone does not tell us exactly where every individual goes.

Use the Arctic tern as a familiar long-distance example if useful, but keep focus on variation within species. Age, sex, condition, competition and resource access may contribute to differences in migration patterns.

MODULE 04 · THE ANNUAL CYCLE

Migration connects places.

  1. Breeding period
  2. Post-breeding movement
  3. Migration and stopovers
  4. Non-breeding period
  5. Return migration

Why stopovers matter

Birds may need places to rest and refuel. Conditions at stopovers can affect the rest of the journey.

The places connected by a migratory route can face very different threats.

Explain the annual cycle as a useful simplification; actual timing varies by species and individual. Introduce migratory connectivity: populations that breed together may use different non-breeding areas, and threats at one point can affect the whole cycle.

MODULE 04 · KNOWLEDGE CHECK

What does the evidence show?

A wetland has many more records of a bird species in November than in May. What can we conclude from those records alone?

Choose an answer to see feedback.

Use this to reinforce the distinction between a pattern and its explanation. Repeated surveys, comparable effort, ringing/resighting or tracking may help establish seasonal movements and possible drivers.

MODULE 05 · WHY MIGRATE?

Why leave a place that works?

A place that is suitable now may not remain suitable throughout the year.

Potential benefits

  • Seasonal food availability
  • Favourable breeding conditions
  • Reduced exposure to harsh conditions
  • Greater reproductive opportunity

Potential costs

  • High energy demands
  • Predation and bad weather
  • Risky stopovers
  • Time and opportunities lost during travel
Migration is not a conscious calculation. Natural selection can favour migratory strategies when their effects on survival and reproduction outweigh the costs relative to alternatives. Different individuals may experience different costs and benefits.

MODULE 05 · THINK LIKE A RESEARCHER

Does food explain migration?

Shorebirds arrive at a coastal wetland each winter after travelling long distances.

Try this: What evidence would help test the hypothesis that seasonal food availability influences their use of the wetland?

Possible evidence
  • Measure prey abundance across seasons and sites.
  • Record bird abundance using comparable survey effort.
  • Measure feeding rates or body condition where appropriate.
  • Compare sites or years with different prey availability.

A large number of birds at a food-rich site is suggestive, but it does not by itself prove food caused migration.

Allow a brief discussion. Ask what other factors might explain the pattern, such as habitat availability, weather, disturbance or the location of breeding grounds. Finish by asking how climate-driven shifts in seasonal resources could affect migratory schedules.

DAY 01 · KEY TAKEAWAYS

Notice carefully.
Ask better questions.

  • Separate observations from interpretations.
  • Use Tinbergen's questions to structure explanations.
  • Define behaviours before measuring them.
  • Migration varies—and patterns need evidence.
  • Benefits and costs help frame hypotheses about why birds move.

Next: turn research questions into field methods.

Close by asking participants to share one idea they will take away. Preview Day 2: how the question determines what to observe, where and when to sample, and which method is appropriate.
