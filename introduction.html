YOU'VE PROBABLY HEARD THIS ONE

Brood parasitism.

The koel lays its eggs in another bird's nest, relying on the host to incubate the eggs and raise the chick.

It's a behaviour many people know about. But knowing the label is only the beginning.

Question to the room: How do we know what happens in a nest we aren't watching all the time?

PEOPLE HAVE BEEN WATCHING BIRDS FOR A LONG TIME

Observation is not new.

More than 2,000 years ago, Aristotle described cuckoos laying eggs in the nests of other birds.

THEN

Describe what happens

Early naturalists recorded behaviour and proposed explanations, sometimes shaped by the beliefs of their time.

NOW

Describe. Test. Revisit.

Scientific study asks what evidence supports an explanation, what alternatives exist, and how we can distinguish between them.

The shift is not from “watching” to “science”. It is from observation alone to systematic questions and evidence.

A MUCH BIGGER STORY

The koel isn't the only one.

Brood parasitism occurs in many cuckoo species, with different hosts and strategies.

A MILESTONE IN ANIMAL BEHAVIOUR

Three scientists. One Nobel Prize.

In 1973, Karl von Frisch, Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries concerning the organisation and elicitation of individual and social behaviour patterns.

01

Karl von Frisch

Famous for research on honeybee communication and sensory abilities.

02

Konrad Lorenz

Known for work on innate behaviour and imprinting.

03

Nikolaas Tinbergen

Helped establish experimental approaches to studying animal behaviour.

Birds featured prominently in this history — including Tinbergen's studies of gulls.

A QUESTION THAT CHANGED THE FIELD

Is behaviour inherited, learned — or both?

Lorenz investigated innate behaviour and imprinting. Other researchers explored learning, experience and development.

Today, we don't treat these as simple opposites. Behaviour can reflect inherited predispositions, learning, development and the environment acting together.

BACK TO THE CUCKOO

Egg ejection

A cuckoo chick ejecting host eggs raises questions about how the behaviour develops, what triggers it, and how it may affect access to parental care.

One behaviour. Several scientific questions.

ONE BEHAVIOUR · FOUR QUESTIONS

Four ways to ask “why?”

01

Function

What are the consequences for survival or reproduction?

02

Development

How does the behaviour develop during an individual's life?

03

Evolution

How has it changed across generations and lineages?

04

Mechanism

How does it work, and what triggers it?

These are Tinbergen's four questions. They complement one another; no single question replaces the others.

WHY STUDY BEHAVIOUR?

Sometimes the applications fly beyond the field.

Homing pigeons

Carrier pigeons, including the famous Cher Ami, carried messages during wartime. Their remarkable homing ability has also motivated scientific questions about orientation and navigation.

V-formations

Studies of birds flying in formation help researchers investigate aerodynamics, flight coordination and the potential energetic benefits of positioning.

These are starting points for scientific questions, not claims that a single bird behaviour directly produced a particular technology.
