MODULE 1 · DEVELOPING A RESEARCH QUESTION

A topic is not yet a research question

TOPIC

Bird song

A broad area of interest.

OBSERVATION

Song varies

Birds appear to sing at different rates in noisy and quiet places.

QUESTION

Is song rate associated with noise?

Names a measurable pattern to investigate.

STUDY

Compare song rate and noise

Collect both measurements using a consistent protocol.

A useful research question identifies the biological system, the variables or process of interest, and the relationship or pattern being investigated.

MODULE 1 · DEVELOPING A RESEARCH QUESTION

Can we answer this question with data?

Too broad

Why do birds sing?

Many possible mechanisms, species and contexts. It is difficult to test as one study.

More researchable

Is dawn song rate associated with ambient noise level in male Indian robins at urban sites?

Specifies a behaviour, a possible explanatory variable, a study system and a context.

A focused question is not necessarily a simple question. It is one for which the required evidence can be identified.

Before collecting data, ask: what observation would count as evidence for an answer, and what result might challenge my expectation?

MODULE 1 · DEVELOPING A RESEARCH QUESTION

Question, hypothesis and prediction are different

?

Research question

Is dawn song rate associated with ambient noise?

The pattern we want to investigate.

H

Hypothesis

Birds adjust their song behaviour in response to acoustic conditions.

A proposed explanation.

→

Prediction

If birds compensate for noise by singing more, song rate will be higher at noisier sites, all else being comparable.

An expected observable outcome.

A prediction should be specific enough that the collected data could be consistent or inconsistent with it. A hypothesis is an explanation, not simply a statement that two variables are related.

MODULE 1 · FROM QUESTION TO STUDY

A bird that leads humans to a hidden resource

Greater honeyguides are known to lead human honey-hunters towards bees' nests, where the hunters can open the nest and access the honey.

The bird can then feed on the wax and other remains. This interaction raises an intriguing question: how does a bird communicate the location of a resource to another species?

An unusual observation is a starting point for scientific inquiry—not yet an explanation of how the behaviour works.

MODULE 1 · FROM QUESTION TO STUDY

What exactly do we want to find out?

OBSERVATION

Guiding behaviour

Honeyguides sometimes lead human honey-hunters towards bees' nests.

PUZZLE

How does it work?

What information does the bird communicate, and how does the human respond?

QUESTION

Does cooperation matter?

Does responding to a honeyguide's signals help a honey-hunter locate a nest?

STUDY

Record the interaction

Measure guiding signals, human responses and whether a nest is located successful.

The shift is from describing something remarkable to identifying a relationship that evidence can help us investigate.

MODULE 1 · FROM QUESTION TO STUDY

Turn the question into a testable prediction

?

Research question

Does responding to a honeyguide's signals improve a honey-hunter's chance of locating a bees' nest?

H

Hypothesis

Honeyguides & human honey-hunters from coordinated behaviour during nest location.

→

Prediction

following guiding signals from honeyguide

=

higher probability of locating a nest

Notice the distinction: the hypothesis proposes an explanation; the prediction states an observable result expected if that explanation is correct.

MODULE 1 · FROM QUESTION TO STUDY

What evidence would help us answer the question?

What would we measure?

  • Whether a honeyguide was present and gave guiding signals
  • Whether the honey-hunter followed those signals
  • Whether a bees' nest was located
  • Time taken and distance travelled before the outcome

What would we need to consider?

  • Experience of the honey-hunter
  • Whether a honeyguide was present and actively guiding
  • Habitat and local availability of bees' nests
  • How guiding and successful nest location are defined consistently

A useful study does not simply collect observations. It records the variables needed to distinguish between possible explanations.

Important: an observational comparison alone may reveal an association, but it cannot automatically show that following the signals caused the difference. Study design determines how strong a conclusion we can draw.

MODULE 1 · DEVELOPING A RESEARCH QUESTION

What exactly will we measure?

Explanatory variable

A variable used to explain or predict variation.

Example: people passing a point per 10 minutes.

Response variable

The outcome measured in relation to the explanatory variable.

Example: proportion of observation time vigilant.

Other variables

Factors that may also influence the response.

Example: vegetation cover, time of day, predator activity.

Operational definition

A rule that makes a variable measurable.

Example: define vigilance as an alert posture with active scanning, not every head movement.

What do these numbers represent?

Each site-year record describes arrival timing at a particular site in a particular year.

However, measurements from the same site across years may be related. Fifty records do not necessarily mean 50 independent sampling units.

The key distinction

Repeated measurements across years help reveal temporal change. Whether sites, site-years or another unit count as replicates depends on the research question and study design.

Sampling across several sites per habitat provides site-level replication and helps distinguish general habitat patterns from site-specific differences.

The key question is not only “How many birds did we observe?” but also “How many independent sites represent each habitat?”

MODULE 2 · DESIGNING A FIELD STUDY

How many things can we measure?

Suppose spotted owlets appear more vigilant in an urban park than in a forest. Vigilance could also be influenced by habitat structure, predator presence, human disturbance, food availability or how accustomed owls are to people.

?

Other influences

  • Habitat structure
  • Predator presence
  • Human disturbance
  • Habituation
+

More variables?

  • More to measure
  • Some hard to quantify
  • Some remain unknown
↗

More sites

  • Independent replication
  • Consistency across sites
  • Stronger generalisation
Key takeaway: We cannot measure or control for every possible influence. Replication across independent sites helps us assess variability and determine whether a pattern can be generalised beyond the sites studied.
Caution: increasing the number of sites does not automatically eliminate the influence of unmeasured variables. Rather, it helps us assess whether the pattern is consistent across independent sites

MODULE 2 · DESIGNING A FIELD STUDY

More observations do not fix missing replication

Pseudoreplication

Treating observations that are not independent replicates as though they provide independent evidence for a hypothesis.

For example, observing many birds in one urban park does not replicate urban conditions across multiple parks.

Replication must occur at the level relevant to the hypothesis. If the question concerns habitat differences, independent sites are essential for separating habitat effects from site-specific effects.

MODULE 2 · DESIGNING A FIELD STUDY

How could we select sites or individuals?

Random sampling

Select units randomly from a defined set.

Useful when: reducing selection bias and making inference to a defined sampling frame are priorities.

Systematic sampling

Select units at regular spatial or temporal intervals.

Watch for: patterns that coincide with the chosen interval.

Stratified sampling

Divide the study area into meaningful groups, then sample within each.

Example: sample urban parks across low, medium and high human-use categories.

Convenience sampling

Sample where access is easiest or observations are readily available.

Limitation: accessible sites may not represent the wider population.

A design can combine approaches. The key is to define the target population, explain how units were selected and recognise the limits of inference.

MODULE 3 · SOURCES OF BIAS AND DETECTABILITY

Not detected ≠ absent

A bird may be present but go undetected: silent, hidden or inactive.

● Presence

Species occurs at the site.

⌕ Detection

Evidence recorded during a survey.

× Non-detection

No evidence recorded ≠ absence.

↻ Repeat surveys

Reveal detections missed on earlier visits.

Example: Visit 1 — not detected · Visit 2 — detected · Visit 3 — not detected.

Repeated surveys help account for imperfect detection and improve estimates of site occupancy when the design and model assumptions are appropriate.

MODULE 3 · SOURCES OF BIAS AND DETECTABILITY

What can we do to reduce bias?

01

Standardise

Use consistent survey duration, timing, equipment and definitions.

02

Replicate

Sample multiple appropriate units and repeat visits where useful.

03

Record context

Document observer, weather, effort, visibility and other relevant conditions.

04

Be transparent

Keep records of exclusions, missing data and protocol changes.

A good design does not eliminate every source of bias. It makes important sources less likely, records what remains, and limits conclusions accordingly.

MODULE 4 · BEHAVIOURAL AND MIGRATION DATA

Movement data also have structure

What might be recorded?

  • Bird identity or tag ID
  • Date and time of encounter or location fix
  • Coordinates or named site
  • Method and detection effort
  • Whether the record is a direct observation, resighting or inferred location

What can it reveal?

  • Seasonal occurrence from repeated observations
  • Connections between sites from marked individuals
  • Routes and stopovers from sufficiently frequent tracking locations
  • Arrival timing and long-term change from comparable records
A map of sightings is not automatically a migration route. To reconstruct an individual route, we need evidence linking locations through time for that individual.

MODULE 6 · FROM IDEA TO RESEARCH STUDY

Case study: how do urban birds respond to people?

Published studies have measured specific behaviours such as foraging rate, alert distance and flight initiation distance in relation to human activity.

Question

Are bird behaviour and escape responses associated with human density in urban parks?

Measurements

Foraging rate, alert distance, flight initiation distance and flight distance.

Design lesson

Measure more than one relevant response: different behaviours may show different patterns.

Interpretation lesson

Differences among sites may also reflect habitat, predator activity or other factors correlated with human density.

Related reading: Prestes et al. (2018), bird responses to human disturbance in urban parks

The variables we choose to measure determine which aspects of a biological response we can detect.

MODULE 6 · A WORKED EXAMPLE

Let's follow one study from idea to evidence

Study idea: nest-building in baya weavers

Male baya weavers build complex nests. We could ask whether the rate of material-carrying trips differs between early and late stages of nest construction.

Question

Does trip rate differ by nest stage?

Hypothesis

Construction stage is associated with how males allocate effort to material transport.

Prediction

Mean trips per standardised observation period differ between stages.

This is an illustrative project framework, not a claim about the actual pattern in baya weavers. The data would determine whether the prediction is supported.

MODULE 6 · A WORKED EXAMPLE

Step 1: define the behaviour before counting it

BehaviourOperational definitionMeasure
Material collectionMale gathers or picks up nest material.Event count, if relevant
Material transportMale carries material towards the nest.Number of trips per observation period
WeavingMale inserts or pulls material through the nest structure.Duration or instantaneous samples
Nest inspectionMale examines or manipulates the structure without adding material.Frequency or duration

An ethogram reduces ambiguity. If one observer counts a short hop carrying fibre as a trip and another does not, the records may not be comparable.

Pilot observations help identify unclear definitions and behaviours that are difficult to distinguish reliably.

MODULE 6 · A WORKED EXAMPLE

Step 2: choose a fair sampling plan

Sampling unit

An individually identified male or nest, with the unit defined consistently.

Replication

Observe multiple nests at each construction stage rather than relying on one nest per stage.

Standardised effort

Use the same observation duration and comparable time windows across nests.

Record context

Note weather, time of day, nest location, mate presence and other plausible influences.

If the same nest is observed repeatedly as it develops, those records are linked. That repeated structure should be reflected in how the data are summarised and analysed.

The illustrative early-stage group has a higher mean. With only three nests per group, we should be cautious and avoid generalising or claiming causation.

MODULE 6 · A WORKED EXAMPLE

Step 4: identify strengths and limitations

Strengths

  • Clear question and measurable response.
  • Standardised observation periods.
  • Behavioural definitions set in advance.
  • Multiple nests observed.

Limitations

  • Small sample size.
  • Other factors may differ among nests.
  • Repeated observations from a nest are not independent nests.
  • Observed association may not establish causation.

A stronger study might sample more nests, follow the same nests across stages, standardise observation conditions and choose an analysis that reflects repeated measures and count data.

A limitation does not make a study useless. It tells us how far its conclusions can reasonably extend and what a future study could improve.

From an idea to defensible evidence

01 Start with a focused research question.
